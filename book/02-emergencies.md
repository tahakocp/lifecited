# 2. Emergencies and first aid

What to do in the first minutes, before help arrives. The UK sources below say "999"; use your own country's emergency number (112 works in the EU and many other places). None of this replaces a first aid course, which takes one day and is the best thing in this chapter.

### 1. Learn the stroke signs: FAST
- Cost: one minute to memorise.
- Gain: face drooping, arm weakness and slurred speech are the main signs; the T is the time to call for help.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Symptoms of a stroke](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/)
  > The easiest way to remember these symptoms is the word FAST. This stands for: face, arms, speech and time to call 999.
  > Symptoms of a stroke can sometimes stop after a short time, so you may think you're OK. Even if this happens, get medical help straight away.
- Note: "it went away" is the trap. Symptoms that pass can be a warning stroke.

### 2. Chest pain that squeezes and spreads: call, don't wait
- Cost: a phone call that may turn out to be nothing.
- Gain: crushing or squeezing chest pain that spreads to the arm, neck or jaw is a reason to call an ambulance.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Heart attack](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/)
  > you have chest pain that's spreading to your arms, neck or jaw
  > chest pain which may feel like crushing or squeezing on your chest

### 3. If someone collapses and isn't breathing normally, push hard and fast
- Cost: learning it once. Practise on a manikin if you can.
- Gain: chest compressions about five to six centimetres deep, 100 to 120 a minute, keep blood moving until help or a defibrillator arrives.
- Grade: C
- Source: [St John Ambulance, How to do CPR](https://www.sja.org.uk/first-aid-advice/cpr/)
  > press down hard, to a depth of about five-six centimetres before releasing the pressure and allowing the chest to come back up.
  > Push at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute.
  > If you are unable or unwilling to provide rescue breaths, give continuous chest compressions.
- Note: send someone for the defibrillator (AED). Public ones give spoken instructions as you go.

### 4. Unresponsive but breathing: recovery position
- Cost: nothing.
- Gain: lying on their side keeps the airway open and lets vomit drain.
- Grade: C
- Source: [St John Ambulance, Recovery position](https://www.sja.org.uk/first-aid-advice/recovery-position/)
  > When someone is put into the recovery position, their airway is kept open and any vomit can drain away without interfering with their breathing.

### 5. Choking adult: 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts
- Cost: nothing.
- Gain: if coughing fails, five sharp back blows, then up to five abdominal thrusts, repeated until it clears or help arrives.
- Grade: C
- Source: [St John Ambulance, Choking](https://www.sja.org.uk/first-aid-advice/choking/)
  > With the heel of your other hand, give them five sharp back blows between their shoulder blades.
  > With your other hand, grasp your fist and pull sharply inwards and upwards up to five times.
- Note: the page has the separate method for babies and children. Use it; abdominal thrusts are not for babies.

### 6. Burns: cool running water for 20 minutes
- Cost: twenty minutes at the tap.
- Gain: cool running water for 20 minutes, within 3 hours, is the first treatment for burns and scalds.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Burns and scalds](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/)
  > Hold the burn or scald under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do this as soon as possible, within 3 hours of it happening.
  > When the burn or scald has cooled, lay cling film over it if you can. Do not wrap the cling film around it.
- Note: not ice, not butter, not toothpaste. WHO gives the same 20 minutes and adds: keep the rest of the person warm.
- Source: [WHO, Burns (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/burns)
  > Cool a thermal burn promptly with cool running water; approximately 20 minutes is commonly advised for smaller burns where this can be done safely.

### 7. Heat exhaustion: cool them within 30 minutes or call
- Cost: shade, water, a fan.
- Gain: heat exhaustion usually settles within 30 minutes of cooling; if it doesn't, treat it as heatstroke.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Heat exhaustion and heatstroke](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/)
  > Heat exhaustion does not usually need emergency medical help if you can cool down within 30 minutes. If it turns into heatstroke, it needs to be treated as an emergency.
  > Cool their skin – spray or sponge them with cool water and fan them.

### 8. Anaphylaxis: use the auto-injector first, then call
- Cost: carrying the injector, always.
- Gain: inject adrenaline straight away, call for an ambulance, and use a second injector after 5 minutes if there is no improvement.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Anaphylaxis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/anaphylaxis/)
  > Use an adrenaline auto-injector (such as an EpiPen) if you have one – instructions are included on the side of the injector.
  > If your symptoms have not improved after 5 minutes, use a 2nd adrenaline auto-injector.

### 9. Know the sepsis warning signs
- Cost: nothing.
- Gain: confusion, slurred speech or not making sense in someone with an infection is a reason to go to emergency care now.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Sepsis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sepsis/)
  > Sepsis is a medical emergency. Call 999 or go to A&E immediately if you think you or your child might have sepsis.
  > is confused, has slurred speech, or is not making sense

### 10. Poisoning: don't make them vomit
- Cost: nothing.
- Gain: making someone sick can make them choke; keep the packet and call for advice.
- Grade: C
- Source: [NHS, Poisoning](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/poisoning/)
  > do not try to make someone sick if you think they've swallowed something harmful
  > do not give the person anything to eat or drink

### 11. Earthquake: drop, cover, hold on
- Cost: one drill a year with the family.
- Gain: get down on hands and knees, cover your head and neck, hold on until the shaking stops.
- Grade: C
- Source: [Ready.gov, Earthquakes](https://www.ready.gov/earthquakes)
  > Drop where you are onto hands and knees.
  > Cover your head and neck with one arm and hand.
