# 1. Don't die early

Cheap things that remove a large share of the risk of dying young: crashes, fire and gas, water, falls, and the slow killers that have no symptoms until late. Most of them cost nothing or a few minutes.

### 1. Wear a seat belt in every seat, every trip
- Cost: nothing. Two seconds per trip.
- Gain: a seat belt cuts the risk of death for people inside the vehicle by up to 50%.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > Wearing a seat-belt can reduce the risk of death among vehicle occupants by up to 50%.
- Note: back seats count. "Up to" means the best case across studies, not a guarantee for every crash.

### 2. Put small children in a proper child seat
- Cost: a seat, new or checked second-hand. Ten minutes to fit it right.
- Gain: child restraints are linked to a 71% drop in deaths among infants.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > The use of child restraints can lead to a 71% reduction in deaths among infants.
- Note: follow the seat's manual for the child's weight and height, and check the fitting after every move between cars.

### 3. Wear a helmet on anything with two wheels and a motor
- Cost: a helmet. Five seconds to buckle it.
- Gain: in a Cochrane review, motorcycle helmets cut the risk of death by 42% and of head injury by 69%.
- Grade: A
- Source: [Liu et al. 2008, Helmets for preventing injury in motorcycle riders (Cochrane review, abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18254047/)
  > helmets were estimated to reduce the risk of death by 42% (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.68) and from six higher quality studies helmets were estimated to reduce the risk of head injury by 69%
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > Correct helmet use can reduce the risk of death in a crash by more than 6 times and the risk of brain injury by up to 74%.
- Note: WHO puts it even higher for correct use. "Correct" means the strap is done up. An open strap or a helmet on the handlebar does nothing.

### 4. Drive a little slower
- Cost: a few minutes per trip.
- Gain: every 1% increase in mean speed adds about 4% to the risk of a fatal crash, so small cuts in speed matter.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > every 1% increase in mean speed produces a 4% increase in the fatal crash risk and a 3% increase in the serious crash risk.
- Note: for people on foot the curve is steep. Being hit at 65 km/h instead of 50 km/h raises the risk of death 4.5 times.
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > The risk of death for pedestrians hit by car fronts rises rapidly (4.5 times from 50 km/h to 65 km/h).

### 5. Don't drive after drinking, even a little
- Cost: a taxi, a friend, or a night without a drink.
- Gain: crash risk starts at low blood alcohol and rises sharply from 0.04 g/dl.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > the risk of a road traffic crash starts at low levels of blood alcohol concentration (BAC) and increases significantly when the driver's BAC is ≥ 0.04 g/dl.
- Note: the legal limit in your country may be higher than 0.04. The legal limit is not the safe limit.

### 6. Put the phone out of reach while driving
- Cost: answering messages later.
- Gain: hands-free calls are not much safer than holding the phone, and texting raises crash risk a lot.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Road traffic injuries (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)
  > Hands-free phones are not much safer than hand-held phone sets. Texting considerably increases the risk of a crash.

### 7. Fit a carbon monoxide alarm near where people sleep
- Cost: a battery or plug-in CO alarm. Replace it when the maker says.
- Gain: CO from heaters, generators and grills kills more than 400 Americans a year outside of fires; you cannot smell it, an alarm can detect it.
- Grade: C
- Source: [CDC, Carbon monoxide poisoning basics](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html)
  > Each year, more than 400 Americans die from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires, more than 100,000 visit an emergency department, and more than 14,000 are hospitalized.
  > Install battery-operated or battery back-up CO detectors near every sleeping area in your home.
- Note: the numbers are for the US only. Never run a generator or a charcoal grill indoors or in a garage.

### 8. Never leave a young child alone near water
- Cost: attention. A fence or a gate if you have a pool or a well.
- Gain: drowning is the fourth leading cause of death for children aged 1–4; barriers and fencing greatly cut exposure.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Drowning (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/drowning)
  > Drowning is the fourth leading cause of death for children aged 1–4 years and the third leading cause of death for children aged 5–14 years.
  > Covering wells, using doorway barriers and playpens, fencing swimming pools and otherwise controlling access to water hazards greatly reduces water hazard exposure and risk.
- Note: bathtubs, buckets and garden ponds count. Swimming lessons help, but do not replace watching.
- Source: [CDC, Preventing drowning](https://www.cdc.gov/drowning/prevention/index.html)
  > Children who have had swimming lessons still need close and constant supervision when in or around water.

### 9. After 65, train balance and strength
- Cost: two or three short sessions a week. Tai chi counts.
- Gain: in trials, exercise cut the rate of falls in older people by about 23%.
- Grade: A
- Source: [WHO, Falls (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/falls)
  > A Cochrane review found exercise reduced the overall rate of falls by approximately 23% in trial populations
- Source: [Sherrington et al. 2019, Exercise for preventing falls in older people living in the community (Cochrane review, abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30703272/)
  > Exercises that target balance, gait and muscle strength have been found to prevent falls in these people.
- Note: also ask a doctor or pharmacist to review medicines that cause dizziness. In the US, falls are the leading cause of injury death after 65.
- Source: [CDC, Older adult falls](https://www.cdc.gov/falls/about/index.html)
  > Falls among adults 65 and older caused over 43,000 deaths in 2024, making it the leading cause of injury death for that group.

### 10. Get your blood pressure measured once a year
- Cost: free at many pharmacies and clinics. Five minutes.
- Gain: about 44% of adults with high blood pressure do not know they have it, and it usually has no symptoms.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Hypertension (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hypertension)
  > An estimated 600 million adults with hypertension (44%) are unaware that they have the condition.
  > The only way to detect hypertension is to have a health professional measure blood pressure.

### 11. If you smoke, get help to stop
- Cost: some weeks of discomfort. Counselling and medication are free in some countries.
- Gain: nicotine replacement (patch, gum, lozenge) raises the quit rate by 50% to 60%; counselling plus medication can more than double the chance.
- Grade: A
- Source: [Hartmann-Boyce et al. 2018, Nicotine replacement therapy versus control for smoking cessation (Cochrane review, abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852054/)
  > NRTs increase the rate of quitting by 50% to 60%, regardless of setting
- Source: [WHO, Tobacco (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/tobacco)
  > Counselling and medication can more than double a tobacco user’s chance of successful quitting.
  > Tobacco kills more than 7 million people each year, including over 1.6 million non-smokers who are exposed to second-hand smoke.

### 12. Drink less alcohol; there is no safe minimum
- Cost: depends on the person.
- Gain: alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen and WHO says no amount is free of health effects.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO Europe, No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health (2023)](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health)
  > Alcohol is a toxic, psychoactive, and dependence-producing substance and has been classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer
- Source: [WHO, Alcohol (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/alcohol)
  > Worldwide, 2.6 million deaths were attributable to alcohol consumption in 2019
- Note: this does not say one drink a week is dangerous. It says the risk starts above zero, so less is better.

### 13. Get the HPV vaccine (children and young adults) and cervical screening
- Cost: free in many national programmes. One short test every few years.
- Gain: cervical cancer is largely preventable with HPV vaccination and regular screening.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Cervical cancer (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cervical-cancer)
  > Cervical cancer is largely preventable through HPV vaccination and regular screening, as recommended by national guidelines, and it can be cured if detected early and treated promptly.
- Note: follow your own country's schedule for ages and intervals.

### 14. Hepatitis B and measles vaccines on time
- Cost: free in most national schedules.
- Gain: the hepatitis B vaccine gives nearly 100% protection; measles vaccination averted nearly 59 million deaths between 2000 and 2024.
- Grade: B
- Source: [WHO, Hepatitis B (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-b)
  > It offers nearly 100% protection against the virus.
- Source: [WHO, Measles (fact sheet)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles)
  > Measles vaccination averted nearly 59 million deaths between 2000 and 2024.
- Note: adults who are not sure they were vaccinated can ask for a blood test or a catch-up dose.

### 15. Start bowel cancer screening at 45
- Cost: a stool test at home, or a colonoscopy, depending on the country.
- Gain: the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for all adults aged 45 to 75.
- Grade: A
- Source: [USPSTF, Colorectal cancer: screening (2021)](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening)
  > The USPSTF recommends screening for colorectal cancer in all adults aged 50 to 75 years.
  > The USPSTF recommends screening for colorectal cancer in adults aged 45 to 49 years.
- Note: USPSTF grades the 50 to 75 group "A" and the 45 to 49 group "B". Other countries use other ages. Blood in the stool at any age is a reason to see a doctor, not to wait for screening.
